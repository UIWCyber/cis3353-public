Every Member Presents

  • Every team member speaks, and each member presents the user stories they personally contributed to.
  • If you did not commit the work, do not present it as yours. The board and the commit history back up who owns what.
  • Say your name and the part you owned, then speak only to your stories.

The Build, Attack, Defend Story

  • Every team walks the same three beats, in order, so scoring stays fair.
  1. 1Build: a diagram plus a live demo of the running environment (VMs and virtual networks up).
  2. 2Attack: demonstrate the threat against the undefended build, so the audience sees the risk is real.
  3. 3Defend: show the defenses in place, then the verification test that proves each one works.

What the Graders Watch For

  • Live demo of the built environment: the VMs and networks actually run; the topology matches your diagram.
  • Attack shown, then blocked: the same attack that worked before defenses now fails, with evidence.
  • Module mapping: each defense is tied to one of your at-least-three integrated modules and its SY0-701 domain.
NoteReward honest security reasoning over a polished slide. "The scan still gets one port, here is why we accepted that risk" shows the deepest learning.

The Peer Feedback Form

  • The audience completes one peer feedback form per presenting team. It is filled in during or right after each talk.
  • Completing the forms is your participation credit for the day, so treat it as part of your own grade.
  • The form is on Canvas: search the course files for "Capstone Peer Feedback Form."
TipWrite one concrete strength and one concrete suggestion per team. "Good job" is not feedback; "the DMZ diagram made the segmentation obvious" is.

During Each Talk

The Missed-Slot Rule

  • A failed live demo is not a missed slot: switch to your recorded video and keep going. Not showing up is the problem.
